Leveraging rare disease genetics to illuminate specialized cell biology

We use molecular biology, biochemistry, and animal modeling to investigate mechanisms underlying human genetic disorders. We are particularly interested in rare Mendelian diseases that dysregulate the biogenesis and function of specialized cells and subcellular compartments.

Research

Our lab is grounded in the belief that rare human genetic disorders can illuminate fundamental principles in cell biology and reveal new treatments for common and uncommon disease alike. These ideas have already paid huge dividends in heart disease and cancer and are growing in scope as medical practice increasingly incorporates molecular genetics.

We are currently most interested in monogenic disorders of proteostasis and organelle quality control. Proteostasis and organellar defects, often manifested by subcellular protein aggregation, are common during aging and across diverse human disease states, yet their molecular basis is hard to uncover because model systems are limited and the initiating events in complex, often age-related disease are poorly understood. We take a genetics-driven approach: we study human syndromes that phenocopy sporadic proteostasis and organellar diseases but have a single defined cause that is amenable to mechanistic work in the laboratory, using cultured immortalized cells, primary human stem cell cultures, and mouse models together with biochemistry, mass spectrometry, advanced microscopy, and functional genomics.

Familial bronchiectasis

Bronchiectasis describes a family of related pulmonary disorders caused by failure of airway mucociliary clearance. In most cases the underlying cause remains unknown and there are few effective therapies. We are leveraging familial forms of the disease to identify genetic mechanisms that initiate and exacerbate bronchiectasis. In earlier work, we showed that loss of a previously orphan protein kinase, NEK10, causes bronchiectasis through defects in the growth and assembly of airway cilia, the specialized organelles that drive mucociliary clearance. We continue to work on the molecular basis of this and other forms of Mendelian airway disease using molecular genetics and biochemistry in airway culture systems.

Hereditary pulmonary fibrosis

Pulmonary fibrosis is an incurable disorder caused by a large, heterogeneous, and poorly understood family of interstitial lung diseases. Rare familial forms are caused by mutations in factors required for the biogenesis and trafficking of specialized organelles unique to the lung, and are accompanied by accumulation of aggregated proteins and lipids within these compartments. We have developed new methods for purifying and analyzing these organelles from genetically engineered mice and are working to understand their biochemical roles in lung disease.

Mendelian neurodegeneration

Pathogenic expansions of GGC trinucleotide repeats cause a family of related neurodegenerative disorders typified by intranuclear aggregates. The composition and cellular consequences of these aggregates were unclear, as was why GGC repeat diseases specifically affect the brain. We developed methods to address these questions and found that polyglycine aggregation sequesters FAM98B and disrupts tRNA processing. We are now working out the molecular details of this process and, more broadly, the role of tRNA dysregulation in human disease.

Team

Raghu Chivukula, MD, PhD

Principal Investigator

Raghu is Assistant Professor of Medicine and Surgery at Harvard Medical School, Associate Director of the Center for Genomic Medicine at Massachusetts General Hospital, and Associate Member of the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard. He attends in the MGH intensive care unit and co-directs the MGH Adult Pulmonary Genetics Clinic.

He trained at Johns Hopkins (BA, MD, PhD), completed internal medicine residency at MGH and pulmonary and critical care fellowship at Harvard, and was a postdoctoral fellow at the Whitehead Institute. His work has been recognized by the Burroughs Wellcome Career Award for Medical Scientists, the Smith Family Award for Excellence in Biomedical Research, and the ASCI Young Physician-Scientist Award. Outside the lab and hospital he can be found trying to convince his kids to go climbing with him and coaxing his 1975 Porsche 911 to start.
